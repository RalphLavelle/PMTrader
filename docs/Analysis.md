## The one-paragraph version

PMTrader is a TypeScript/Express API that reads Jupiter prediction-market data and
runs it through a LangGraph Swarm of five specialist agents. There is no
supervisor: agents hand control to one another through `transfer_to_*` tools and
share results through LangGraph state channels. The LLM decides routing and reads
the market's prose; deterministic TypeScript decides every number, veto, and
position size. Completed runs persist to MongoDB. The research stack (collector →
features → labeller → backtest → fitness) now grades **intramarket trading**:
would a decision made while a market was open have been profitable if exited later?
Final resolution remains useful context for calibration, but it is not the primary
scoreboard.

## What Phase 4 measured

The offline backtest (`api/src/backtest/`) replays collected market history through
the swarm's own decision logic and grades each decision as an **intramarket trade**:
enter at the decision quote, hold for a configured window, exit at a later quote.
Markets do not need to have resolved to be graded — they need enough open-path
snapshots for an exit.

**Primary metrics:** profitable share, return on stake, total mark-to-market PnL.  
**Secondary:** resolution accuracy / calibration when a label exists.

Its first structural finding about the architecture still stands:

**The quantitative core almost never trades on its own.** Trend and Mean Reversion
are deliberate opposites, and `aggregateSignals` multiplies the blended confidence
by how much the specialists agreed. Replaying a *synthetic* market priced at 78% YES
with healthy depth, the two return confidences of 0.639 and 0.627 pointing opposite
ways, and the blend collapses to **0.006** — two orders of magnitude below the 0.55
approval bar. On a milder market Mean Reversion abstains instead, but
`meanConfidence` still divides by the neutral signal, halving the survivor.

Two consequences carried into Phase 5:

1. The swarm's directional opinions come largely from the **Semantic Agent**, which
   the backtest deliberately cannot replay (it is non-deterministic, costly, and its
   input prose was never collected). The measurable part of the system is therefore
   not the decisive part of the system.
2. Grading the *blend* is close to useless while the blend always holds. Grading each
   **specialist's stance as a trade** is not, and the backtest already records those.

## What Phase 5 added

Agent fitness (`api/src/fitness/`, dashboard at `/data/fitness`) grades the **swarm
and each specialist on coherent paper trades** from intramarket replay. Suggested
weights are driven by trading skill (win rate on completed buy→sell cycles) when
the sample is large enough. Resolution Brier / "did they pick the winner?" remain
as secondary forecasting diagnostics.

`GET /api/fitness/weights` serves the adaptive weight table Phase 6 applies in
`aggregateSignals` (current/baseline weight, P&L, drawdown, regime, evidence).

## What Phase 5 found, and how it corrects Phase 4

Running it against the current dataset produces 40 graded stances across 10 resolved
markets, and every single one is **neutral**. Not a disagreement that cancelled out —
no opinion at all.

That is a meaningful correction to how the Phase 4 finding should be read. The 0.006
annihilation above came from a *synthetic* market constructed to have a price and
depth. On the actual collected data the specialists never reach that stage:

| What the report shows | Why |
|-----------------------|-----|
| 100% of stances on markets with no usable quote | Those markets were first collected *after* they stopped trading, so `deriveFeatures` fell back to an even 50/50 |
| Both agents 100% neutral | `priceExtremity` of 0 sits below Trend's 0.06 threshold, so it correctly declines to call a direction |
| Skill vs market exactly 0.000 | The benchmark was equally uninformative — both the agents and the price said "50/50" |
| 0% of stances with a stored order book | The orderbook stream only covers the watchlist; these markets came from the wide sweep |
| 100% of graded markets resolved NO | The answer key is entirely one-sided, so accuracy is meaningless here regardless |

So the honest summary is that **the sample is degenerate, not the agents**. Phase 4's
structural criticism of `aggregateSignals` still stands as a criticism of the blending
rule, but nothing in the live dataset has tested it yet, and the fitness report now
says so in its own `warnings` array rather than leaving a reader to infer it from a
table of zeroes.

The semantic agent contributed nothing either, for a separate and more encouraging
reason: 21 swarm runs are stored and 14 of them have usable prices, but the labeller
has not yet matched any of their markets to a decisive result. That resolves itself
with time rather than with code.

## What has to change before any of this means anything

The bottleneck is not the maths. It is coverage, and specifically one kind of
coverage:

1. **Markets watched while open.** A market first seen after it closed can never be
   graded, by either Phase 4 or Phase 5. This is the single constraint that gates
   everything downstream.
2. **Order book coverage on those same markets.** Depth feeds Trend's imbalance term
   and the liquidity score; without it, two of the three inputs to every confidence
   are effectively zero.
3. **A balanced answer key.** Ten markets that all resolved NO cannot distinguish a
   skilled agent from one that says NO reflexively.
4. **Resolved markets among the stored runs.** Until then the semantic agent — the
   component supplying most of the swarm's directional opinion — stays ungraded, and
   it is the one Phase 6 would most want a weight for.

`docs/Overview.md` reaches the same conclusion from the other direction: runs come
back "hold" because no real-world evidence flows into the Semantic Agent. Phases 4 and
5 confirm the numeric half of that story — even with perfect price data, the blending
rule as written would suppress most directional views, and the data available so far
has not been good enough to put a number on how much that costs.
